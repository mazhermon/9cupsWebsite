# Design Inspiration Reference Guide

This skill is your visual compass. Before creating or modifying ANY design, you must review the reference images stored alongside this file. They represent the look and feel the user wants to achieve across all creative output.

## How to use this skill

### Step 1: Review references before every design task

Every time you are about to create or modify a visual design, read the relevant reference images from the `references/` folder next to this SKILL.md. Do not skip this step, even if you think you remember what the references look like from a previous task. Fresh eyes on the references each time will keep your output aligned.

```
references/
├── general/          ← Broader design inspiration (typography, layouts, color, composition)
└── project-specific/ ← 9cups brand materials and direct style references
```

**Always review project-specific references first** — these are the most direct representation of the target aesthetic. Then review general references to absorb the wider design language the user admires.

To review, use the `Read` tool on each image file. You don't need to read every single image every time — but you should read at least 5-8 project-specific references and 5-8 general references to refresh your sense of the visual direction. If the task is closely related to a specific project (e.g., album art, music promo), lean heavier on project-specific. If the task is more general (e.g., a website layout, presentation), lean heavier on general references.

### Step 2: Identify which patterns apply to the current task

After reviewing the references, pause and think about which visual qualities from the references are most relevant to the task at hand. Consider:

- **Color palette**: What tones and color relationships from the references fit this piece?
- **Typography style**: What type treatments from the references would work here?
- **Composition approach**: How are elements arranged in the references that relate to this layout?
- **Texture and layering**: What overlay, blending, or textural techniques from the references apply?
- **Overall mood**: What emotional tone do the references convey that this piece should echo?

### Step 3: Apply the aesthetic to your work

As you create the design, actively channel what you absorbed from the references. Don't just glance at them and forget — let them shape your decisions about color choices, font styles, layout structure, and visual effects.

## Aesthetic DNA — What the references tell us

The following is a distillation of the visual patterns present across the reference collection. This section exists to help you quickly orient yourself, but it is NOT a substitute for looking at the actual images. Always review the reference files.

### General Design Language

The general references showcase work from contemporary creative studios, portfolio sites, and editorial design. Key patterns include:

**Typography**: Bold, oversized, often sans-serif type that commands attention. Letters sometimes break the grid, overlap images, or get cropped at edges. There's a confidence to the type — it's not decorative, it's structural. Think display-weight fonts used at scale, sometimes with creative spacing or unconventional line breaks.

**Color**: Two modes recur — deep, dark palettes (blacks, navy, charcoal) with high-contrast accents, and bold saturated fields of single colors (red, orange, electric blue). White space is used deliberately. When color appears, it's committed — not pastel or tentative.

**Layout**: A mix of clean grid-based structure and deliberate rule-breaking. Asymmetry is common. Negative space is treated as a design element, not leftover room. Photography and type interact — they overlap, bleed, and share space rather than sitting in separate boxes.

**Mood**: Confident, contemporary, editorial. The work feels like it belongs in a creative studio or music magazine — not corporate or overly polished, but intentional and cool.

### 9cups Project-Specific Language

The project-specific references reveal a distinct and recognizable visual identity:

**Color palette**: Purple, violet, and magenta are the dominant tones — used as photographic overlays, solid backgrounds, and gradient washes. These purples range from deep plum to bright magenta. Warm accents of coral, salmon, and pink complement the purple family. This purple-to-pink gradient spectrum is a core brand signature.

**Photography treatment**: Photos are rarely left natural. They receive color-shifted overlays (purple/magenta tints), double exposures, transparency effects, and color channel manipulation. The result feels dreamy, slightly psychedelic, and musically evocative — like seeing through tinted glass or stage lighting.

**Illustration style**: Hand-drawn white line illustrations appear on purple backgrounds — loose, gestural figures of people dancing and moving. The line work is energetic and informal, not precise or geometric. These illustrations are a strong brand element.

**Brand mark**: The 9cups logo combines the Roman numeral IX with a chalice/cup symbol, referencing the Nine of Cups tarot card. The tarot card itself (Rider-Waite style) appears in the references as a foundational brand image.

**Subject matter**: Music performance, DJ culture, live events, studio sessions, beach/outdoor lifestyle. The imagery connects to the world of independent music production and live electronic/DJ performance.

**Texture**: Grain, noise, and analog imperfections are embraced. The aesthetic leans toward warmth and tactility rather than pixel-perfect digital cleanness. Think film photography processed through digital color manipulation.

## Applying references to different design types

### Album/single cover art
Lean heavily on project-specific references. Use the purple/magenta color overlay technique on photography. Consider incorporating the hand-drawn dancing figures or the 9cups logo mark. The mood should feel like late-night music — warm, immersive, slightly hazy.

### Web design / landing pages
Pull from both general and project-specific references. Use the bold typography approaches from the general references, but apply the 9cups color palette (purples, magentas, warm accents). Dark backgrounds with high-contrast type work well. Let photography breathe and interact with the layout.

### Social media graphics / promotional materials
Combine the bold type confidence from general references with the purple color treatment from project-specific. The hand-drawn dancing figures on purple backgrounds make excellent social assets. Keep things punchy and immediate.

### Presentations / documents
Use the color palette and typography confidence from the references, but adapt for readability. Dark slide backgrounds with light type, or white backgrounds with purple accents. Don't overdo the photo manipulation effects in contexts where clarity matters.

### Posters / print materials
This is where you can go all-in on the aesthetic. Large-scale type, dramatic color overlays, layered photography, hand-drawn elements. Reference both collections heavily and push the visual intensity.

## Checklist before delivering any design

Before finalizing any visual output, quickly verify:

1. Did I review reference images from the `references/` folder before starting?
2. Does the color palette align with what the references show?
3. Does the typography approach echo the boldness and style of the references?
4. Does the overall mood feel consistent with the reference collection?
5. If this is 9cups-specific work, did I incorporate the signature purple/magenta palette and visual treatments?

If you can answer yes to all of these, the design is on track.
